    What is this?

    This is the Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwien theorem: any two polygons of equal area can be cut into a finite number of pieces that, only slid and turned, rebuild each other. Offcut does not illustrate that proof, it carries it out on whatever you type or draw. Both shapes are cut into simple cells, turned into rectangles and recut into bars of one height. Bars of equal total area push together into the same ribbon, so the ribbon can be cut along both shapes' lines at once.

    Words and drawings are curved, so they are first traced into straight-sided polygons. A circle therefore becomes a polygon with many sides, and is only approximately squared: with scissors and finitely many pieces, squaring a true circle is impossible.

    The theorem needs equal areas. That is why the second shape is scaled, uniformly and about its centre, until its area matches the first.

    In three dimensions the same statement is false. A cube cannot be cut into finitely many pieces that rebuild a regular tetrahedron of the same volume (Max Dehn, 1900).
