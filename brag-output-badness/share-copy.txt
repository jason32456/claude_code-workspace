Every browser you have ever used breaks paragraphs greedily: longest line that
fits, then it forgets. TeX solves the whole paragraph at once.

I implemented Knuth–Plass total-fit line breaking in a tab — and then used TeX
itself as the referee.

\tracingparagraphs=1 doesn't report TeX's answer, it reports its reasoning:
every feasible breakpoint it considered, with the badness, demerits, fitness
class and running total of the line arriving there. Nothing is left to infer,
so a disagreement is a bug rather than an opinion.

On Knuth's own example paragraph it comes back identical, down to the final
figure of 3535. Across the committed oracle: 423 lines, no difference anywhere.
5,986 words hyphenated exactly as \showhyphens does, zero spurious breaks.
387 word widths exact to the scaled point — 1/65536 of a point.

TeX found four bugs I could never have seen, because every one of them produced
a paragraph that looked completely fine:

· the interword space was 1sp too wide, because TFM fixed point doesn't round
  the way arithmetic does
· every "va" pair was 18204sp too wide, because the first matching lig/kern
  instruction wins and the second was overwriting it
· hyphenation silently widened words, because splitting "officer" destroys the
  ffi ligature underneath it
· the last line was charged 10100 demerits for a forced break TeX charges
  nothing for

It also reports where it loses. At a narrow measure, greedy leaves fewer
visibly bad lines than optimal does — the algorithm can't avoid bad lines in a
narrow column, only stop them being catastrophic. Rivers were supposed to be a
clean secondary win. They aren't.

No build, no dependencies, no network. Runs from its own folder.
